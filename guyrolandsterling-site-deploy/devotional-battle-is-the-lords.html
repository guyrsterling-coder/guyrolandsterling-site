The Battle Is the Lord's
1 Samuel 16-18
Two Titans

To really understand this story, we have to understand the two people who met on that battlefield — because neither of them was ordinary, just in very different ways.

The Philistines' champion was named Goliath. He had been a soldier since he was a boy. He stood six cubits and a span — roughly 9'9" tall — so enormous that the Bible pauses to describe his armor piece by piece: a bronze helmet, bronze scale-armor weighing somewhere between 125 and 150 pounds, a bronze javelin between his shoulders, bronze plates covering his legs. His spear was the size of a weaver's beam, and the iron spearhead alone weighed roughly 15 pounds. It's easy to understand why an entire army was frightened to death of him.

Israel's champion was just a boy, named David. I say "just a boy" on purpose — because Israel's whole future was about to rest in his hands.

About three thousand years ago, before David ever wore a crown, he was a shepherd boy, watching his father's sheep in the hill country outside Bethlehem. It doesn't sound like much of a training ground for a fight with a giant who'd been raised for war. But it was exactly that.

We tend to picture David wandering peacefully across green hills. The truth was much harder than that. In ancient Israel, a family's sheep were its wealth — its food, its clothing, the animals it offered to God. Handing a flock to a son wasn't done lightly. It took years to earn.

That training started early. A boy of five or six would tag along with his father or older brothers, just watching — learning how sheep behaved, how they answered a shepherd's voice, how the older men cared for them. He'd be trusted with the newborn lambs first, and the injured ones, because those required patience and a gentle hand more than strength.

By around ten or twelve, once he'd proven he could be trusted, a boy might be given a portion of the flock to watch on his own — venturing further out, staying away longer, eventually through entire nights alone. Scripture never tells us David's exact age when Jesse first trusted him with the flock by himself, but this was very likely the season of his life when it happened.

From there, David's real education began. Alone in the hills, he learned to find good pasture and dependable water. He learned to guide sheep with his voice instead of force. He learned to treat a wound, pull a thorn, help a ewe through a difficult birth.

But shepherding demanded more than gentleness. It demanded courage.

Every shepherd carried two tools: a rod, a short heavy club for fighting off predators, and a staff, long and curved, for guiding sheep and pulling them out of trouble. And just as important — a sling. Not a toy. A shepherd's long-range weapon, practiced for so many hours that a boy could hit almost anything he aimed at. A well-thrown stone could turn a wandering sheep, scare off a jackal, or, if it came to it, kill a wolf, a lion, or a bear.

David would later tell King Saul exactly that: "Thy servant kept his father's sheep, and there came a lion, and a bear, and took a lamb out of the flock... and I went out after him, and smote him" (1 Samuel 17:34-35). Not one lucky moment. The result of years of practice that started when he was a child.

Life out there taught survival, too. When a shepherd camped far from home, he'd build a makeshift fold out of stones or thornbushes, and once the sheep were inside, he would lie down across the one narrow opening himself — becoming, quite literally, the door. Anything that wanted in had to go through him first.

Which makes it worth pausing on something Jesus says, much later: "I am the door of the sheep" (John 10:7). The people listening to him understood that picture immediately. They had seen shepherds do exactly that with their own eyes.

By the time Samuel anointed him, David was still young. But he already carried years of discipline, patience, courage, and quiet trust in God — none of it built in a palace. All of it built alone, in the hills, with sheep.

Two titans, then. One enormous in body. The other enormous in faith. This is the story of what happened when they finally met.

I. The Boy Nobody Called

In the town of Bethlehem lived a man named Jesse, and he had eight sons. One day, the prophet Samuel came to Jesse's house, sent by God to choose the next king of Israel. Jesse lined up seven of his sons in the yard, and Samuel looked them over one by one.

The oldest, Eliab, was tall and strong, with the kind of face that looked like it belonged on a king. Samuel thought, surely, this must be the one. He reached for his horn of oil, ready to pour it out.

But God stopped him. Man looks on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart (1 Samuel 16:7).

Not Eliab. Samuel moved to the next son. Not him either. And the next, and the next — seven sons in all, and God said no to every single one.

Samuel turned to Jesse. "Are these all the sons you have?"

And Jesse said something strange. "There is still the youngest. He's out in the fields, watching the sheep." Jesse hadn't even thought to bring him in.

So they sent for David.

Here's something worth knowing while they waited: God had already chosen David, long before this day. Years earlier, God told Samuel he was looking for "a man after his own heart" (13:14) — and no one else in the whole Bible is ever called that. Not Abraham. Not Moses. Only David.

When David finally walked in — dusty, sunburned, years younger than every one of his brothers — Samuel didn't hesitate. "Arise, anoint him: for this is he" (16:12).

Samuel poured the oil over David's head, right there in front of his brothers. And then — nothing happened. No parade. No crown. No throne. David just walked back out to the sheep, like any other day, carrying a secret nobody else knew: he had just been chosen to be king, and his whole life still looked exactly the same.

Except that everything had changed. "The Spirit of the LORD came upon David from that day forward" (16:13).

And more than once, that quiet job turned dangerous — the lion, the bear, the fights nobody else ever saw. David never called it his own strength. "The LORD... delivered me out of the paw of the lion, and out of the paw of the bear" (17:37) — God saved him, not his own hands.

We don't know much about how David's family treated him at home. The one clue we get isn't a kind one — later on, his oldest brother speaks to him with real contempt (17:28). But here's what we do know for sure: David was the youngest of eight boys, given the lowest job in the family, left out of the room the one day his father assumed only the older sons would matter.

Whatever his family thought of him didn't matter in the end. On the one day it truly counted, David was the one standing there.

II. Forty Days, One Stone

Every morning, a voice rolled down into the Israelite camp — huge, echoing off the hills on both sides. It was Goliath, and this wasn't just noise to scare people. He was calling out actual terms: "Choose you a man for you, and let him come down to me. If he be able to fight with me, and to kill me, then will we be your servants: but if I prevail against him, and kill him, then shall ye be our servants, and serve us" (17:8-9). Whoever won this one fight decided the future of the entire nation. Lose, and it wasn't just one soldier's life on the line — it was everyone's freedom. Every man, woman, and child in Israel would belong to the Philistines.

Every evening, the voice came back. This happened for forty days in a row.

By now, the fear didn't feel new anymore. It just felt normal, like bad weather everyone had learned to live under. All Israel "were dismayed, and greatly afraid" (17:11) — not just a few nervous soldiers, the whole army. Even King Saul, taller than every other man in Israel (9:2), stayed hidden in his tent.

One morning, a boy walked into that camp carrying bread and cheese. His father had sent him to check on his older brothers — nothing more. But while he was there, David asked a simple question: what happens to the man who finally beats this giant?

That question made his oldest brother furious. "I know thy pride," Eliab snapped, "and the naughtiness of thine heart; for thou art come down that thou mightest see the battle" (17:28).

Word got back to King Saul, and he sent for David. "Thou art not able to go against this Philistine to fight with him," he said, "for thou art but a youth" (17:33) — and in a real sense, Saul was right. Israel's law said fighting men had to be twenty years old (Numbers 1:3), and David had never been a soldier.

It helps to remember David's relationship with Saul's court wasn't yet official or constant. Sometime earlier, Saul's servants had recommended David as a musician for the times the king's spirit troubled him — even calling him, with maybe a little too much enthusiasm, "a mighty valiant man, and a man of war" (16:18). Saul grew fond of him. But David kept going back and forth the whole time, tending his father's sheep whenever the king didn't need him (17:15), and he likely only came to play music on the days Saul's spirit troubled him. He wasn't yet a permanent fixture in the palace; that came later, after this very battle, when Saul finally "took him that day, and would let him go no more home to his father's house" (18:2). Enough time may have passed, with David out in the hills the whole while, that he simply looked different too — the same way any boy can change a great deal in just a year or two. Their relationship, up to this point, had only been occasional.

David didn't argue with Saul's doubt. He just told him the truth.

"Thy servant kept his father's sheep, and there came a lion, and a bear, and took a lamb out of the flock: And I went out after him, and smote him, and delivered it out of his mouth: and when he arose against me, I caught him by his beard, and smote him, and slew him. Thy servant slew both the lion and the bear: and this uncircumcised Philistine shall be as one of them, seeing he hath defied the armies of the living God." David said moreover, "The LORD that delivered me out of the paw of the lion, and out of the paw of the bear, he will deliver me out of the hand of this Philistine" (17:34-37).

That was the whole of it. No résumé. No military record. Just a boy standing in front of a king, asking to be trusted with the fate of an entire nation — the biggest thing he had ever asked for in his life — with nothing to offer as proof except two fights nobody else had ever seen, out in a field, with no one watching.

Think about what Saul actually agreed to next. This wasn't a friendly contest with nothing at stake. Saul was about to bet the freedom of his entire kingdom — every soldier, every family in Israel — on a shepherd boy that no one in that army had ever seen fight. If David lost, it wouldn't just cost David his life. It would cost everyone theirs. Picture the soldiers standing there, watching their king hand the nation's whole future to the least likely person in the camp. Whatever David actually said to convince him, it must have been remarkable — enough to move a king to risk everything on a boy nobody else would even consider.

Saul agreed. He put his own armor on David — heavy bronze, a big helmet, a sword too large to swing. David took a few steps and stopped. He pulled it all back off. He hadn't tested that armor. He wasn't going to fight wearing somebody else's protection (17:39).

Instead, he took his staff in his hand, and chose him five smooth stones out of the brook, and put them in a shepherd's bag which he had, even in a scrip; and his sling was in his hand (17:40) — five stones, not just one. Maybe because, years later, we learn Goliath had four brothers who were also giants (2 Samuel 21:15-22). Maybe David was ready to fight the whole family that day, if it came to that.

Then Goliath stepped out — armored exactly as everyone had described, exactly as fearsome as the whole camp had come to dread.

He walked closer, his shield-bearer out in front of him, until he was close enough to actually see who Israel had sent. And when he saw David — just a boy, ruddy-faced, more handsome than fearsome — he was insulted. He'd expected a real fight. Instead, it looked to him like a mockery.

"Am I a dog," Goliath shouted, "that thou comest to me with staves?" He cursed David by his own gods, right there in front of both armies. "Come to me," he said, "and I will give thy flesh unto the fowls of the air, and to the beasts of the field" (17:42-44).

David didn't flinch, and he didn't trade insults. He answered with something far bigger than either of them: "Thou comest to me with a sword, and with a spear, and with a shield: but I come to thee in the name of the LORD of hosts, the God of the armies of Israel, whom thou hast defied" (17:45). Then he handed Goliath's own threat right back to him — not just to Goliath, but to his whole army: "This day will the LORD deliver thee into mine hand... that all this assembly shall know that the LORD saveth not with sword and spear: for the battle is the LORD's, and he will give you into our hands" (17:46-47). He wasn't only talking to Goliath. He was making a promise in front of two entire armies, about who God really is — and staking his life on it being true. The old word he used for "deliver" didn't mean handing something over politely. It meant to shut, the way a door closes and locks. David wasn't hoping he might win. He was saying it was already decided. God has already delivered — shut — you into my hand.

He swung the sling around — once, twice, faster and faster — and let it fly. It crossed the open field in less time than it takes to breathe and struck Goliath right in the forehead, the one place his armor didn't cover. The giant who had terrified a nation for forty days fell all at once. David ran to him, took the giant's own sword, and finished it. The Philistine army, watching their champion fall, turned and ran — and the Israelite army, no longer afraid, chased after them.

III. Years Later

Years later, once David was serving in Saul's royal court, the Bible drops in one more detail, almost like a side note: "David behaved himself more wisely than all the servants of Saul" (18:30). Not the best fighter in the palace. Not the most talented musician. The wisest person there — outthinking men who had grown up inside the palace, trained by the palace — and David had never spent a single day being taught there. His whole education happened in a field, with sheep, and God, for company.

David didn't become a different person the day he faced that giant. He simply became visible. Everything that made it possible had already been built, years earlier, in a place nobody was watching.

You will probably never have to face a real giant. But you have your own version of that quiet field — the ordinary days when nothing seems to be happening and no one is watching.

The real question this story leaves us with isn't whether you'd be brave enough to face a giant.

It's this: what's being built in you right now, in secret, for a day you don't know is coming yet?
