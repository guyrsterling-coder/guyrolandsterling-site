Reflections
Song of Solomon
Eden to Calvary
By Guy Roland Sterling

There are books of Scripture that confront us with law. Others confront us with judgment. Some lead us through history, prophecy, suffering, wisdom, or doctrine.

Then there is the Song of Solomon. It does something different — it brings us into the room. We hear the voice of one searching for the one she loves. We feel longing before reunion, beauty through the eyes of the beloved, footsteps at the door. Hesitation, absence, desire, delight, the fear of loss, and the relief of being found again. Nothing distant. Nothing cold. Love here is near, personal, powerful, dangerous, beautiful — and Scripture does not apologize for any of it.

The Song lets us feel love before it explains anything about it. That may be the first lesson: love is not first encountered as a definition. It is experienced — a voice, a presence, a longing, someone who matters so deeply that their absence changes the entire room.

“My beloved is mine, and I am his.” — Song of Solomon 2:16

Few words, yet almost everything about covenant love is already there. Not merely I love him. Not merely he loves me. Belonging — I am his, he is mine. Love has crossed beyond attraction into possession without ownership, surrender without bondage, closeness without the loss of personhood. Two lives have become bound to one another.

When Love Awakens

Three times the Song gives nearly the same warning: “Do not stir up, nor awaken love, until it pleases” (Song of Solomon 2:7). Why warn us? Because love is powerful. Fire is good in the hearth, not in the field. Water gives life — it can also sweep a man away. Love awakened at the wrong time, in the wrong place, toward the wrong person, or without the willingness to bear its weight can leave wounds that last for years.

Scripture does not treat love casually, and neither should we. The world often speaks of love as though the feeling itself justifies whatever follows. The Song does not. It tells us there is a proper time — desire must be governed, longing must be honored. The heart is not a toy. Neither is another person. Love reaches deep places, and once awakened, it cannot always be put back to sleep. That is why the Song does not merely celebrate passion. It teaches reverence for it.

The Garden

The language of the Song is filled with gardens, fruit, fragrance, fountains, spices, vineyards, flowers, trees, and running water. The imagery is not accidental — it brings us back toward Eden. Before shame. Before accusation. Before a husband blamed his wife, before a wife knew fear, before desire became corrupted by selfishness, there was a man and a woman together, unashamed.

The Song lets us glimpse something of that lost world — not perfection; there are still moments of fear, delay, separation, searching, and sorrow. But throughout the book there remains this desire for unguarded closeness. The beloved says, “A garden enclosed is my sister, my spouse, a spring shut up, a fountain sealed” (Song of Solomon 4:12).

A garden enclosed — not abandoned, not trampled, not open to everyone passing by. Protected, treasured, entered only through trust. Biblical intimacy is never common; what is given to one is not given to all. Love creates a place where another person is permitted to enter because covenant has made that place safe. This is why adultery wounds so deeply — it does not merely break a rule. It enters a garden that belonged to another.

Love Searches

The Song is not one uninterrupted celebration. There is absence. One night the woman searches for the man she loves: “By night on my bed I sought the one I love; I sought him, but I did not find him” (Song of Solomon 3:1).

Anyone who has deeply loved understands those words. The bed has not changed. The walls have not changed. The night has not changed. Yet everything feels different because someone is missing. She rises, searches the streets, asks the watchmen. She will not rest until she finds him.

Later, a scene becomes even more painful. Her beloved comes to the door. She delays. Then she rises — too late. “I opened for my beloved, but my beloved had turned away and was gone” (Song of Solomon 5:6).

Love can miss its moment. A word left unsaid. A door not opened. An apology delayed. Affection assumed, another day taken for granted — we imagine love will always be there tomorrow exactly as it was today, and Scripture refuses to let us believe that. Love must be tended. The person beside you is not furniture, not background, not guaranteed by habit. Love notices. Love answers. Love opens the door.

Love Matures

Something subtle happens as the Song develops.

“My beloved is mine, and I am his.” — Song of Solomon 2:16

“I am my beloved’s, and my beloved is mine.” — Song of Solomon 6:3

“I am my beloved’s, and his desire is toward me.” — Song of Solomon 7:10

The order changes. At first the thought begins with him belonging to her. Then it begins with her belonging to him. Love matures. At first we often ask: Do you love me? Will you stay? What will you give me? Will you make me happy? But mature love begins to ask something else: What can I give? How can I remain faithful? How can I protect what has been entrusted to me? How can I become a place of rest for the one I love?

Love moves from possession toward surrender — not losing oneself, but giving oneself. That difference is enormous.

Love Tested

Centuries later, Paul writes to believers in Corinth. The church was gifted, yet divided — people competed, compared, boasted, demanded recognition. Paul begins speaking about spiritual gifts, then suddenly says there is “a more excellent way.” Love. Not romance, not sentiment, not poetry — love under pressure. The Song shows us love awakened. Paul shows us love tested.

“Love suffers long and is kind; love does not envy; love does not parade itself, is not puffed up.” — 1 Corinthians 13:4

Now love has entered ordinary life. Someone has irritated you. Someone has been praised while you were overlooked. Someone has disappointed you, spoken carelessly, failed to understand you, taken more than they gave. What does love do then? It suffers long. It remains kind. It does not envy or make itself the center. It is not easily provoked, and it does not keep a private ledger of every injury. Suddenly love becomes much harder than desire — anyone can feel affection when affection is returned, anyone can be tender when everything is easy. Paul takes love out of the garden and places it under weight. Now we discover whether it is love at all.

Paul goes further: love “does not seek its own” (1 Corinthians 13:5). Those words expose us. Much that we call love is actually desire for something from another person — attention, affirmation, companionship, security, praise, loyalty, comfort. We may genuinely care for someone and still quietly build the relationship around what we receive. Then the person grows tired, gets sick, becomes distracted, fails us, cannot give what they once gave — and the question emerges: did I love the person, or did I love what the person gave me? That does not mean love has no needs. It means love does not make itself the measure of everything.

Love “bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things” (1 Corinthians 13:7). Paul is not describing blindness — love does not call evil good, does not excuse abuse, does not deny truth; he has already said love “rejoices in the truth.” But true love does not flee merely because it has become expensive. It bears. It hopes. It endures.

This brings us directly back to the Song: “Many waters cannot quench love, nor can the floods drown it” (Song of Solomon 8:7). Floods come. Every covenant eventually meets water — time, aging, financial trouble, misunderstanding, illness, temptation, loss, disappointment, change. Seasons when one gives more than the other; moments when affection feels effortless, and others when faithfulness becomes a decision. Yet Scripture says there exists a kind of love the waters cannot extinguish. Paul names it: “Love never fails” (1 Corinthians 13:8). The Song says water cannot quench it. Paul says it does not fail. Different voices, same fire.

Strong as Death

Then the Song reaches its height.

“Set me as a seal upon your heart, as a seal upon your arm; for love is as strong as death.” — Song of Solomon 8:6

A seal marked ownership, identity, and authority. Put me there, she says — not merely near you. Upon your heart, where your deepest affections live. Upon your arm, where your strength is seen. Let love govern both affection and action.

Then comes the astonishing statement: love is as strong as death. Death is relentless. It takes kings and servants, rich and poor, strong and weak. No locked door finally keeps it out, no wealth buys exemption, no human power defeats it. And the Song places love beside it. Strong as death. Love also lays claim. Love also refuses to loosen its hold. Love also reaches where power cannot.

And now Scripture has brought us somewhere beyond human romance — because only one love has actually met death face to face and conquered it.

Christ

Paul says love does not seek its own. Then we read, “Even Christ did not please Himself” (Romans 15:3). Paul says love bears all things. Christ bore the cross. Paul says love endures all things. Christ endured rejection, humiliation, scourging, nails, shame, and death. Paul says love never fails. The grave could not hold Him.

Now Song of Solomon 8 begins to open before us in a way human marriage alone cannot fulfill. Love is strong as death — at Calvary, love entered death. The One who loved gave Himself for the beloved. Not because she had earned Him. Not because she had become beautiful enough. Not because she had first loved perfectly.

“God demonstrates His own love toward us, in that while we were still sinners, Christ died for us.” — Romans 5:8

This is love under its greatest weight — not loving the lovable, but loving the guilty; not giving from abundance, but giving Himself; not remaining until suffering begins, but remaining through death.

Jesus called Himself the Bridegroom. John the Baptist called Him the Bridegroom. Paul speaks of the Church as promised to one Husband. Revelation ends with a marriage. Suddenly the entire Bible seems to move toward a wedding — from a man and woman in a garden, through covenant, through betrayal, through redemption, until finally, “the marriage of the Lamb has come, and His wife has made herself ready” (Revelation 19:7).

Scripture begins with a wedding. Scripture ends with a wedding. Between them stands a cross, and that cross tells us what kind of love carries the story from the first garden to the last — not sentiment, not attraction, not mere emotion, but sacrificial covenant love. The Bridegroom gives Himself for His Bride. “Christ also loved the church and gave Himself for her” (Ephesians 5:25). Love and giving cannot finally be separated. Where love is deepest, giving follows.

The Song continues: “If a man would give for love all the wealth of his house, it would be utterly despised” (Song of Solomon 8:7). Love cannot be purchased. A man can buy a house, jewels, land, clothing, comfort — he cannot buy another person’s heart. A woman may receive everything a man possesses and still never love him. Love must be freely given. That truth takes us again toward God. He could command obedience — He does command obedience — but love cannot be reduced to command. This is why Christ’s question to Peter after the resurrection is so piercing: “Do you love Me?” (John 21:15). Not do you fear Me. Not will you obey Me. “Do you love Me?” Because love gives willingly what law alone can never produce: the heart.

Examine Love

“Set me as a seal upon your heart.” That line deserves to remain with us. What has been sealed upon your heart? Whose approval, whose memory, whose rejection, whose affection? What fear, what desire, what treasure? Christ said, “For where your treasure is, there will your heart be also” (Matthew 6:21). The heart always reveals what it treasures. The Song asks for love to be sealed there. Paul tells us what that love must become. Christ shows us its perfect form: patient, kind, truthful, self-giving, enduring, faithful through suffering, stronger than death.

It is easy to ask whether someone loves us. It is harder to ask whether we love — not whether we feel something, but whether we love. Am I patient when love costs me time? Kind when I have been wounded? Free from envy when someone else is praised? Able to rejoice in truth even when truth corrects me? Do I seek the good of the one I claim to love, or do I keep a record of wrongs and quietly bring it out when needed? Do I endure only while the relationship serves me, or has love become something deeper?

These questions are not merely for husbands and wives. They reach every relationship — family, friendship, church, neighbor, enemy — and finally Christ. Because the question eventually becomes unavoidable: do I love Him? Not merely His promises. Not merely salvation, not merely heaven, not merely what He gives. Him.

Every reader eventually enters deep water. No one escapes. Loss comes, people leave, bodies weaken, plans fail, words wound, years move faster than expected. Some things we thought permanent disappear. And in those waters, much that we once called love is exposed — some affection disappears when reward disappears, some loyalty disappears when circumstances change, some promises survive only while they remain convenient. But Scripture tells us there is another kind. Many waters cannot quench love. Love never fails. And then, Christ — a cross, a grave, an empty tomb. Love entered the flood. Love entered death. Love remained.

The Greater Love

Jesus said, “Greater love has no one than this, than to lay down one’s life for his friends” (John 15:13). The Song teaches us that love awakens. Paul teaches us that love endures. Christ shows us that love gives itself. That is the journey. Desire says, I want you. Covenant says, I belong to you. Mature love says, I will remain faithful to you. Christ says, I give Myself for you.

Now the words of the Song become almost unbearable in their depth: “Set me as a seal upon your heart.” The believer does not merely place Christ upon the heart as a pleasant thought. Christ becomes the treasure, the reason, the life — and the love shown at Calvary begins shaping the love given to others. Patient because He was patient. Forgiving because we were forgiven. Faithful because He is faithful. Giving because He gave. Enduring because He endured. Loving because He first loved us.

Song of Solomon begins in longing. It ends in fire. Paul begins with gifts and ends by showing something greater than every gift. Christ begins with God loving the world and walks willingly toward a cross. Together they show us what love is — not weakness, not sentiment, not possession, not appetite, not words alone. Love sees. Love gives. Love waits. Love protects. Love tells the truth. Love bears. Love remains. Love sacrifices. Love does not quit when the waters rise.

And at its highest, love is not merely something God commands. It is something God revealed — in Christ, at the cross, for us.

Love is as strong as death.

But in Christ—

Love became stronger.
